“Understanding” isn’t a single thing in the brain (or in an AI). It’s what it feels like when a bunch of smaller, cheaper processes snap together into a model that can *predict, explain, and guide action* across many situations.

Here’s a useful way to see how it emerges.

## 1) Prediction comes first

Brains (and many learning systems) are constantly trying to anticipate what comes next: the next sound in a sentence, the next step in a task, the consequence of an action.

When your internal model predicts well, you experience the world as “making sense.” When it predicts badly, you get surprise/confusion—your model has to update.

So “understanding” is tightly tied to: **being able to compress experience into a model that predicts**.

## 2) Compression creates concepts

Raw experience is huge and messy. To cope, systems look for reusable patterns:

* Many different chairs → “chair”
* Many different social situations → “politeness,” “status,” “awkwardness”
* Many different physics events → “momentum,” “friction,” “support”

These concepts are *compressed summaries* that keep what matters for prediction and discard what doesn’t. The better the compression, the broader the understanding.

## 3) Hierarchies build meaning

Understanding tends to become layered:

* **Low-level features:** edges, tones, letters
* **Mid-level chunks:** words, objects, familiar actions
* **High-level structure:** goals, causal stories, strategies, identities

Each layer reuses the one below it. This is why you can read a sentence without thinking about every letter: higher layers “explain” lower-level details efficiently.

## 4) Causality and counterfactuals are the big jump

A lot of “I get it” moments happen when you move from correlation (“this tends to happen”) to causal structure (“this happens *because*…”).

A strong sign of genuine understanding is counterfactual reasoning:

* “If I remove this support, it will fall.”
* “If the incentive changes, behavior changes.”
* “If I tweak this variable, the outcome changes.”

That’s more than pattern-matching; it’s an internal simulator.

## 5) Action closes the loop

Understanding isn’t just a map—it’s a map that lets you *do* things:

* Choose actions that achieve goals
* Avoid costly mistakes
* Generalize to new situations

A model that predicts but can’t guide action often feels like “knowing facts.” A model that predicts *and* supports planning feels like “understanding.”

## 6) Social language helps “lock in” understanding

Humans don’t just learn from direct experience. We inherit models through language: explanations, analogies, textbooks, stories.

Language does two key things:

* It lets you import other people’s compressed models.
* It forces your own model to become *explicit and checkable* (you can test it by explaining it).

This is why teaching something often deepens understanding: you’re stress-testing your model for gaps.

## 7) The “click” feeling is a coordination event

That sudden sense of clarity is often when multiple partial models become consistent:

* a new concept links to old ones
* predictions improve across many cases at once
* fewer exceptions are needed
* you can answer “why?” and “what if?” smoothly

Subjectively: it feels like a snap.
Mechanistically: it’s a reorganization that reduces error and increases reuse.

## Practical ways to tell you understand something

Try these. If you can do most of them, your understanding is robust:

1. **Explain it simply** without hand-waving.
2. **Predict outcomes** in new examples.
3. **Generate counterexamples** and say why they fail.
4. **Answer “what if”** changes.
5. **Teach it** and handle questions.
6. **Use it to make decisions** (even small ones) and see it work.
