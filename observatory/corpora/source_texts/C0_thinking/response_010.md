“Understanding” isn’t a single switch that flips on. It’s what it feels like (and what we can observe behaviorally) when a system builds **usable, compressive, generalizable models** of the world—and can deploy them flexibly.

Here are the main pieces people think are doing the work.

## 1) Compression turns experience into concepts

Brains (and many AI systems) are prediction machines under constraints: limited time, energy, memory. So they’re pushed to **compress** incoming data into patterns that explain a lot with little.

* If you can describe many observations with a small internal code, you’ve effectively found a **concept**.
* “Dog” is a compression of many sensory episodes into a reusable latent structure: four legs, certain faces, barks, behaviors, etc.

Understanding starts to appear when the compression is **structured**, not just a bag of correlations.

## 2) Prediction error drives learning (surprise is the teacher)

A common story in neuroscience is **predictive processing**: the brain constantly predicts what it’s about to perceive, compares prediction to reality, and uses the mismatch (prediction error) to update its model.

* Repeatedly reducing prediction error builds internal models that track stable regularities.
* But crucially: good models don’t just predict *what usually happens*—they learn **what would happen if…** (counterfactuals).

That “what if” capability is a big marker of understanding.

## 3) Abstractions emerge as latent variables

Raw sensory data is messy. To make it manageable, systems learn hidden factors—**latent variables**—that explain the variation.

Examples:

* Vision: lighting vs. shape vs. material
* Language: intent vs. literal words
* Social: beliefs vs. actions

When a system separates these factors (often called **disentangling**), it can recombine them in new ways—another hallmark of understanding.

## 4) Compositionality lets you build new thoughts from old parts

Understanding becomes powerful when representations are **compositional**: smaller elements can be combined systematically.

* If you understand “red” and “square,” you can understand “red square” without needing to have seen that exact pairing.
* In humans, language supercharges this: words become handles for reusable mental pieces.

Systems that can compose tend to generalize better and feel more “understanding-like.”

## 5) Causal models replace mere associations

A big shift is moving from “X correlates with Y” to “X causes Y (under these conditions).”

Causal understanding gives you:

* **Intervention**: “If I change X, Y changes.”
* **Explanation**: “Y happened because X.”
* **Robust generalization**: works when the environment shifts.

In humans, a lot of understanding is basically building and refining causal stories.

## 6) Grounding and action make models meaningful

Understanding isn’t just having a model—it’s being able to **use** it.

When internal representations connect to:

* perception (what’s out there),
* action (what I can do),
* goals/values (what matters),

…they become *grounded*. This is why doing, manipulating, experimenting, and getting feedback accelerates understanding—your model gets tested against reality.

## 7) “Aha!” moments are phase transitions in the model

Insights can feel sudden even though learning was gradual. That can happen when:

* enough small updates accumulate that a simpler rule suddenly explains everything,
* or a new abstraction/latent variable “clicks” and reorganizes many facts at once.

It’s like adding the right coordinate system: nothing changes outside, but everything becomes easier to describe and predict.

## 8) Social understanding emerges from modeling other minds

Humans also build models of *agents*—what others want, know, and believe (often called theory of mind).

This kind of understanding leans heavily on:

* simulation (“what would I do in their shoes?”),
* inference from behavior,
* language and shared norms.

It’s especially “emergent” because it’s recursive: I model you modeling me.

## 9) Why it looks “emergent” rather than engineered

Understanding often appears emergent because it’s not a single module—it’s the interaction of:

* learned representations (compression),
* prediction and error-correction,
* memory and retrieval,
* attention (what to process),
* planning (using models to choose actions),
* and language (building portable abstractions).

When these parts reach a certain maturity and connectivity, capabilities can show **nonlinear jumps**: suddenly you can explain, transfer, analogize, and solve new problems.
